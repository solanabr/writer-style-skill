# Why stablecoins are eating emerging-market FX

The oldest savings technology in Latin America is a hundred-dollar bill in a drawer: folded into a book, taped behind a picture frame, dead weight that has outperformed every peso account in the country. Ask around São Paulo, ask in Buenos Aires: someone in the family keeps one. It looks paranoid. Then you sit at a kitchen table and actually run the numbers with someone (I have; it's not an abstraction there), and it starts to look like the most rational trade of the decade.

The principle inside that drawer is the whole piece: when a currency melts, people don't go looking for a better app. They go looking for a different currency to hold.

Why should you care, reading this from somewhere with a boring, stable currency? Because the drawer is where crypto quietly found its first product with everyday, undeniable demand, and reading the stablecoin numbers as an FX story instead of a crypto story is the difference between seeing the next decade of adoption coming and being genuinely surprised by it.

So here's the route. First, the melt: what actually happened to the peso and the real. Then what Brazil's famous 90% number is really buying. Then why PIX, Brazil's beloved instant-payments rail, isn't even playing the same game. Then the corridor money. And at the end, what I'd watch to know if I'm wrong.

## The melt

Across 2019–2024, the Argentine peso lost more than 90% of its dollar value. That's the cleanest example on the continent, so make it a round number: a stack of pesos worth $1,000 in 2019 bought less than $100 worth of dollars by 2024. Its holder did nothing wrong. No bad trade, no hack, no scam: they just… held.

That's why USDT in Argentina isn't a bet. It's the drawer. A widely used, boring savings vehicle, adopted by people who would laugh if you called them crypto enthusiasts. Enthusiasm has nothing to do with it; they're hedged.

Brazil runs a softer version of the same weather. The real traded roughly between 4.8 and 6.2 to the dollar across 2020–2025, and I lived every leg of that ride; watching the dollar value of everything you've saved swing that much while you touch nothing rewires what the word "saving" even means. It's not Argentina, not close. It just teaches the same lesson at a gentler slope: the unit leaks.

## What the 90% is buying

Now, the number that made me want to write this. Brazil's central bank has said publicly (this is Campos Neto, in 2024 statements) that roughly 90% of Brazilian crypto flows are stablecoins. Stablecoins, if the term is new to you, are tokenized dollars: the drawer, moved into a phone.

Sit with that 90% for a second. To a first approximation, an entire country's crypto activity is people buying dollars. Not tickers, not strategies. Dollars. And the shelf they're buying from is deep: supply is dominated by USDT and USDC, whose combined float sits north of $250 billion by 2025 reporting (an approximation, but the order of magnitude is the point).

Small confession: it took me too long to say any of this out loud, because I used to file stablecoins under "the boring corner of crypto." Guilty, big time. The boring corner turned out to be the product. The emerging-market use case is dollar access without a US bank account: a savings hedge against local-currency depreciation on one side, a cheap cross-border settlement rail on the other, and neither half needs you to care about crypto at all. Everything else in the industry is still auditioning. This thing already has the job.

## PIX won a different war

So why would a Brazilian, with a world-class payments rail already in their pocket, ever touch a stablecoin? This is where visiting analysts get confused, and where honestly a lot of pitch decks faceplant. Brazil already solved instant domestic payments: the central bank shipped PIX in November 2020, a central-bank rail, free and instant, and it worked so well that "send me a PIX" replaced cash, cards, and half of everything else in daily life. Credit where it's due, the BCB out-shipped the entire fintech industry. Which makes the question sharper, not weaker.

Because PIX moves reais. Instantly, for free, beautifully, inside Brazil, denominated in the currency you watched leak a section ago. PIX answers the question "how does money move here?" about as well as that question can be answered, which is exactly why it's the wrong lens for stablecoins, because they exist to answer the two questions PIX was never built to touch: what should my money be while it sits, and how does it cross a border.

Stablecoins are not competing with PIX. Different problem. A country can completely solve domestic payments (Brazil basically did) and leave dollar access exactly as closed as it was before. The domestic-payments story and the dollar-access story only look like the same story if you've never needed the second one.

## The corridor money

Cross-border is where the numbers get loud. Latin America and the Caribbean pull in remittance inflows on the order of $150 billion a year, per World Bank reporting from the 2023–2024 era. The global average cost of a traditional remittance corridor runs about 6%. Walk one round example with me: a cousin in Miami sends $200 home. At that average, about $12 evaporates along the way in fees and FX spread before the family sees a cent. Money earned in night shifts and weekend shifts, shaved every single month, forever.

Now run the same $200 as a stablecoin transfer on Solana. It settles in seconds, and the rail itself costs a fraction of a cent — the base fee is 5,000 lamports per signature, a number too small for a bank statement to even display. Seconds versus days. A fraction of a cent versus about $12. That's not a cheaper corridor; it's a different category of thing.

Honesty check, because this is where I'd be lying by omission: the rail fee is not the whole cost. Getting local currency into a stablecoin and back out (the on-ramps and off-ramps) still carries spread and paperwork, and that's where the real friction lives today, and, if you're a builder reading this, exactly where the opportunity lives too. The chain solved its leg of the trip; the edges are still being built. Even so, the gap between "about 6%" and "a fraction of a cent" is wide enough that the edges can stay imperfect for years and the math still wins.

## What to watch, and how I'd know I'm wrong

Zoom out and the shape is simple. Stablecoin adoption is strongest exactly where local currencies are weakest, because the thing being adopted was never really crypto: it's the dollar, minus the drawer. And the next product on that shelf is already visible. FX-hedged, dollar-denominated yield you can hold from a phone is something traditional retail banking in these markets simply does not offer regular people, and for my money it's the biggest open opportunity in emerging-market DeFi. The builders closest to it are the ones building for their own families.

A thesis you can't break isn't a thesis, so here's what would break mine. Watch for local currencies holding their dollar value for a full decade: no melt, no drawer, no demand. Watch for local banks shipping cheap retail dollar accounts with real yield, which would eat the use case from the inside. Watch for the old corridors dropping from about 6% toward zero, which would close the remittance gap without touching a chain. Watch those three. If any of them fire, stablecoins in these markets shrink back into a niche, and honestly I'd be half glad, because cheaper corridors and stronger currencies help the exact same families stablecoins are helping today, just without the detour. The drawer has decades of evidence behind it, though. I don't see the weather changing soon.

If you follow markets, file stablecoins in your FX tab — the crypto tab was always the wrong drawer for them. And if you build, come build the edges: the demand curve here isn't a pitch-deck hypothesis, it's your neighbor's savings. Tell me in the comments what I got wrong (or which corridor you'd want walked through next). What a time to be alive!
