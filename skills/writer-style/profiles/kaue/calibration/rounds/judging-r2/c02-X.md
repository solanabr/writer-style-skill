# Why stablecoins are eating emerging-market FX

Be it an election year, a commodity cycle turning, or a central bank having one of its creative years, the ending reads the same for anyone earning in an emerging-market currency: the money you saved is worth fewer dollars than the money you made. Stablecoin adoption is strongest exactly where local currencies are weakest, and that correlation is not some mystery waiting for a research grant — it's just the demand curve.

Brazil's central bank put a number on it that I still think about: roughly 90% of the country's crypto flows are stablecoins, per the BCB's own 2024 statements (Campos Neto, on the record). In a market known for its noise, nine-tenths of the flow is people quietly buying dollars.

To see why, you need one model, and it fits in a sentence: a currency has two jobs — it moves and it sits, payments and savings, pipes and a bucket. The whole story of stablecoins in Latin America is that Brazil built the best pipes in the business while the bucket kept leaking, so that's the route we'll walk: the pipes first, then the bucket, then the toll you pay to move money between buckets in different countries, and finally what gets built once somebody hands you a bucket that holds.

## The pipes are world-class

Every few months someone lands in São Paulo with a deck about crypto fixing payments for Brazilians, and every few months a Brazilian settles the bar tab over PIX before the pitch is even done. PIX is the central bank's own instant-payment rail, launched in November 2020, free and instant, and it did to domestic payments what people keep promising blockchains will do to everything else, which is to say it simply won. Nobody here is waiting for crypto to fix payments. Payments are fixed.

Which is exactly what makes that ~90% figure so interesting. If stablecoins were a payments play, Brazil (home to arguably the best domestic payment rail anywhere) should be the market where they matter least, and instead it's the market where they dominate crypto flows. So the demand has to be for something PIX doesn't sell, and PIX, for all its genuine brilliance, only ever sells one currency.

What does PIX not sell? Dollars.

## The bucket leaks in real time

So let's talk about the bucket, because this is the part the payments story hides. The real traded roughly between 4.8 and 6.2 to the dollar across 2020–2025. I earn partly in dollars and live in reais, so I've watched that chart with a frequency I would not describe as healthy (my browser autocompletes the ticker after one letter, which is probably its own kind of chart). A swing that wide doesn't just annoy importers, it quietly reprices your rent, your laptop, your groceries, everything with a dollar hiding somewhere in its cost structure, while your salary stays denominated in the thing doing the swinging.

Argentina is the same story with the volume turned all the way up: the peso lost more than 90% of its dollar value across 2019–2024. More than 90%. Five years. Argentines didn't need a whitepaper to understand USDT as a savings vehicle; the use case walked in through the front door and sat down at the kitchen table.

And notice what kind of failure this is, because it matters for what actually fixes it. The pipes are innocent: a depreciating currency moves through them just fine, arguably faster, since nobody wants to be the one still holding it at the end of the month. The failure lives in the sitting. The moment your money has to wait (between paychecks, between invoices, between now and the tuition bill in February), it starts quietly paying rent to whichever macro force is having a moment, and no payment rail, however instant and however free, has anything to say about that.

Strip the branding and this is what stablecoins actually are: tokenized dollars, overwhelmingly USDT and USDC, with combined supply on the order of $250 billion-plus as of 2025 reporting. The pitch to an emerging-market saver is almost embarrassingly boring: the oldest financial product in the world, a dollar that sits still, held from a phone, no US bank account required, finally available to people the dollar system never onboarded. (Fine, there's a little ideology in there too.) The pipes move money; stablecoins are the bucket that holds it.

## A ~6% toll on a $150 billion bridge

Now connect two buckets in different countries and you meet the toll booth. Remittance inflows to Latin America and the Caribbean run on the order of $150 billion a year, per World Bank-era reporting, and the global average cost of a traditional remittance corridor sits around 6%, which means the ugly napkin math lands somewhere near $9 billion a year in tolls, paid disproportionately by the people least able to spare it, for the privilege of moving their own money to their own families at 1990s speeds.

Make it concrete with round numbers. Say a worker abroad sends $200 home every month. At that ~6% average, roughly $12 of every envelope evaporates in fees before it lands, month after month, compounding into something like $144 a year quietly handed to intermediaries for what is, mechanically, an update to two databases. Nobody budgets for that line item. It just gets absorbed, the way tolls always are, by the person with the least room to absorb it.

Against that, the crypto-side numbers stop sounding like marketing. The same $200, sent as a stablecoin transfer on Solana, settles in seconds, and the network's cut is a fraction of a cent — the base fee is 5,000 lamports per signature, the kind of figure you need scientific notation to complain about. And I want to be careful here, because the honest trade-off is real: the transfer is the cheap part. Getting from pesos or reais into stablecoins and back out again, the on-ramp and the off-ramp, is where the friction and the fees still live, and anyone telling you the whole corridor costs a fraction of a cent is selling you the middle of the bridge while hoping you don't ask about either end.

But the middle of the bridge used to be the expensive part, and now it costs effectively nothing, which means whatever remains of that ~6% is a business problem, not a physics problem, and business problems attract competitors.

## What you build once the bucket holds

Here's where the two stories come together. PIX solved money moving inside Brazil; stablecoins solve dollars sitting still and crossing borders; and the opportunity for builders lives in the seam between those two rails, because confusing them produces bad products in both directions. Pitch a "crypto PIX" and the room will politely ignore you, since the central bank already shipped that and shipped it well. Build for the legs PIX doesn't cover, the dollar leg and the cross-border leg, and you're building where there is no incumbent at all.

The clearest product sitting on that seam is one traditional retail banking in these markets has simply never offered: FX-hedged, dollar-denominated yield, accessible from a phone. Your local bank will happily sell you a savings product in the currency that spent the decade losing ground; a dollar account with real yield was reserved for corporates and the already-wealthy. That gap is a market, and it is an enormous one. The builders I keep running into at Superteam Brazil are working exactly on this seam — payroll that lands in USDC, ramp infrastructure that squeezes the expensive ends of the bridge, savings products designed for people who think in grocery bills rather than basis points.

And I'll say plainly what would prove me wrong, because a macro take without a failure condition is just astrology with better charts. Watch the real: if it holds a tight band for a decade, or if local banks start offering retail customers insured dollar accounts with yield, the bucket problem dissolves and stablecoin demand here shrinks into the niche the critics already believe it is. Watch that ~90% flow number too: if it falls while the currency stays weak, then I've misread the demand and people were here for the casino after all. Nothing in the last five years of either chart points that way, but that's the tripwire, and you should hold me to it.
