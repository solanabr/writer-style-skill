# Why stablecoins are eating emerging-market FX

Picture a saver in Buenos Aires. She gets paid in pesos, spends less than she earns, and tucks the difference away every month, exactly what the personal-finance books tell her to do. Across 2019–2024 the peso lost more than 90% of its dollar value. Run that through her account: the discipline was real, the balance even grew, and the purchasing power evaporated anyway while she held it. Not because she picked bad investments. Because the bucket her savings sat in has a hole in the bottom.

Keep that image, it carries the whole piece: money you *hold* is a bucket, money you *move* is plumbing, and the two fail separately.

Why should you care if you're reading this from somewhere with a boring, stable currency? Because this is where real crypto adoption comes from, the kind measured in salaries protected rather than tokens flipped, and if you follow markets (or build products) you want to see it before it shows up in headlines. Here's the route: first why the leak drives adoption wherever currencies are weakest, then what a stablecoin actually buys a saver, then why Brazil's PIX, world-class as it is, doesn't touch this problem, then the cross-border leg, and finally what I'd watch to know if I'm wrong.

## Savings in a bucket with a hole

Argentina is the loud case. When a currency loses more than 90% of its dollar value in five years, holding it is a decision, and most people quietly decide not to. USDT there is widely used as a savings vehicle, a place to stand still while the floor moves. The pattern is boring on purpose: payday arrives, the month's expenses stay in pesos, whatever's left moves into USDT the same day. The saver isn't speculating. She's bailing water.

Brazil is the quiet case, and honestly the more interesting one. No collapse, just a slow pinhole: the real traded roughly between 4.8 and 6.2 to the dollar across 2020–2025. I've lived on this side of it, earning in reais and watching that range reprice everything imported or dollar-denominated. And the flows land exactly where you'd expect: the central bank has said publicly (Campos Neto, in 2024 statements) that roughly 90% of Brazilian crypto flows are stablecoins. Sit with that number. Roughly 90% isn't a speculation profile; it's a savings profile. The dominant on-chain behavior in the country is, of all things, holding dollars.

## A second bucket: the tokenized dollar

So what exactly did those savers buy? A stablecoin is a tokenized dollar, a token on a public blockchain built to track the dollar, and the supply is dominated by USDT and USDC, together on the order of $250B+ by 2025 reporting. For an emerging-market saver the pitch is one line: dollar access without a US bank account.

That line matters because the old FX question was never "do people want dollars." Everyone wants the bucket that doesn't leak. The question was who's allowed to hold one — and the traditional answer ran through a US bank account, or a local dollar product wrapped in paperwork and minimums. A tokenized dollar skips the queue: a phone, an internet connection, done.

It does two different jobs, and keeping them separate is the key to the whole FX story. Job one is standing still: a savings hedge against local-currency depreciation, the second bucket itself. Job two is moving: a cheap cross-border settlement rail, plumbing that carries dollars and doesn't much care about borders. Traditional FX bundled both jobs behind the same paperwork; the tokenized dollar hands both to the same person with no queue at all, and next to asking a banker for permission the difference is not subtle.

Name the trade-off, though, because there is one. You're swapping your local bank's risk for the issuer's: that "dollar" is only as good as the reserves and the redemption promise behind it. Custody moves to you, and if you lose the keys nobody resets your password. The regulation around all of it is still settling. A second bucket, with its own ways to crack.

## PIX fixed the plumbing, not the bucket

Here's where smart people trip, and I'll confess I tripped first: early on I pitched crypto to Brazilian friends as the fix for payments. Blank stares (rightfully so), because Brazilian payments already work. PIX, the central bank's rail, launched in November 2020, free and instant, and it's now simply how money moves here; splitting a dinner bill settles before the waiter comes back. Pitching "fast, cheap payments" in Brazil is pitching ice to Alaska.

So if PIX is that good, why are Brazilians still buying stablecoins? Because PIX moves reais brilliantly and does exactly nothing about what those reais are worth next year. It's plumbing, superb plumbing, moving water between local buckets, every one of them with the same pinhole. It also stops at the border. Stablecoins aren't competing with PIX, and they'd lose if they tried; the domestic-payments war is over. The two legs PIX doesn't cover, holding dollars and crossing borders, are the entire stablecoin lane. It's a wide lane.

There's a bullish read hiding in that, too: PIX proved that when a rail is clearly better, this country adopts it at national scale, fast. The appetite for better money infrastructure isn't in question. The bucket problem is just still open.

## Carrying water across the border

The cross-border mechanics are blunt. Remittance inflows into Latin America and the Caribbean run on the order of $150B a year, per World Bank reporting from the 2023–2024 era, and the global average cost of a traditional remittance corridor sits around 6%: a relay of intermediaries, each one billing for touching the transfer along the way. That toll lands on exactly the people least able to absorb it, workers wiring money home, and it's charged again on every transfer, forever.

Now the same job as a stablecoin transfer on Solana: it settles in seconds and costs a fraction of a cent, the base fee being 5,000 lamports per signature. I won't pretend the whole journey is solved — you still need to get local currency in and out at each end, and that last mile is real friction, still. But on the transfer itself? Seconds and a fraction of a cent against ~6% skimmed off the top. The gap is orders of magnitude wide, too wide to argue otherwise.

## What to watch (and how I'd know I'm wrong)

This ran longer than I planned, so, quick recap:

1. Where the local currency leaks, savings migrate into tokenized dollars. Argentina is the loud version of this; roughly 90% of Brazilian crypto flows being stablecoins is the quiet one.
2. PIX solved domestic payments, free and instant, since November 2020. It never claimed to fix what the bucket holds. Different problems.
3. Cross-border, a ~6% average toll now competes with a rail that settles in seconds for a fraction of a cent. Tolls that wide don't survive better plumbing.

And because a macro take you can't falsify is just vibes, here's the dashboard. Watch the BCB's number: if that roughly-90% stablecoin share of crypto flows decays while the real stays weak, the savings thesis is weaker than I think. Watch combined USDT and USDC supply: continued growth past that on-the-order-of-$250B mark says the demand is still arriving. Watch traditional corridor pricing: if the ~6% average collapses toward nothing, stablecoins lose their loudest argument, and honestly I'd celebrate, cheaper remittances were always the point. The cleanest way to prove me wrong would be years of genuinely strong local currencies: if the leak stops, the second bucket matters less. I'm not betting on that, but I'd happily lose that one too.

What keeps me building here is the product nobody's finished yet: FX-hedged, dollar-denominated yield you can hold from a phone, a thing traditional local banking in these markets does not offer retail savers, period. The first teams to ship that well are serving demand that already exists, in a region that already proved, with PIX, that it adopts better rails at national speed. For a saver in São Paulo or Buenos Aires, that product is a game-changer, and it's sitting right there waiting to be built. It's not that hard to matter here.
